  Why "noun, verb, adjective" is the wrong axis

  Parts of speech are a syntactic categorization, not a semantic one. They classify forms — what role a word plays in a
  sentence's grammatical structure — not what idea the word participates in. Two examples to make the gap concrete:

  - "The signing of the treaty ended the war." Three nouns ("signing," "treaty," "war"), one verb ("ended"). The actual
  concepts are something like EVENT(treaty-signing) → CAUSED → STATE-CHANGE(war-ended). None of the concepts correspond
  to a single noun. "Signing of the treaty" is one concept (an event), and it's spread across a verb-derived noun and a
  determiner phrase.
  - "He ran." One noun, one verb. The concept is an event with an agent and a manner. It happens to fit cleanly inside
  two parts of speech because the sentence is short, but only because English packs more meaning into verbs than some
  languages — in Mandarin you might need three syllables to express the same concept, in Russian one inflected verb
  form. Parts of speech are a syntactic surface feature whose granularity changes from language to language. Concepts
  shouldn't.

  So if "sentence" was wrong because sentence boundaries are syntactic artifacts, "part of speech" is wrong for the same
   reason but worse — it's syntactic and finer-grained than concepts actually are. You'd be cutting concepts into
  smaller syntactic pieces and ending up with something even less meaningful than sentence chunks.

  The Meta paper actually walks past this in its limitations section. They note that "long sentences could reasonably be
   considered as several concepts" and that "any finer splitting of such sentences does not necessary separate well
  these concepts." Translated: smaller syntactic units don't help. The right granularity isn't smaller — it's different.

  So what is a concept?

  Here's my honest position: a concept is a region of the input whose meaning is internally coherent and externally
  separable, and neither edge of that definition is intrinsic to the bytes. Both edges depend on context, attention, and
   what the listener cares about. Three observations that I think are load-bearing:

  1. Concepts are variable-granularity by nature

  Some concepts fit in a single morpheme. "Fire!" is one concept — threat-imperative-flee. So is "Mom." So is "Yes."
  Other concepts span paragraphs of dense exposition where every clause is necessary to convey the single underlying
  idea. Mathematical proofs are like this; legal contracts are like this; the setup of a joke is like this.

  This is why any fixed-granularity unit is doomed. Sentence is too coarse for "fire" and too fine for a proof. Words
  are way too fine for almost everything. Paragraphs are too coarse for fast-moving dialogue and too fine for a 10-page
  essay that develops one argument. Whatever the right unit is, it has to be allowed to be as small as a syllable or as
  large as a chapter, and the size has to be chosen per region of input, not globally.

  This rules out almost every approach I can name, including the LCM. It doesn't rule out attention-based discovery,
  because attention naturally produces variable-width regions of high relevance — the same way engram's retrieve()
  produces ranked spans of variable length.

  2. Concepts are listener-relative

  Here's the part that makes the whole problem genuinely hard: the same input contains different concepts for different
  listeners. A doctor reading a patient's chart and a billing clerk reading the same chart are extracting completely
  different concept structures from the same bytes. The doctor sees symptoms, differentials, treatment-history. The
  clerk sees procedures, codes, dates-of-service. Neither reading is wrong. The bytes don't have intrinsic concepts; the
   bytes have affordances, and which affordances become concepts depends on what the listener is doing.

  This is uncomfortable for anyone hoping for a clean architectural definition, because it means concept boundaries are
  not a property of the input alone. They're a property of the (input, listener-state) pair. The boundaries shift when
  the goal shifts. A model doing summarization carves the input into different concepts than the same model doing
  question-answering.

  The good news is that this is actually how attention already works. Attention is the mechanism by which a query
  creates a relevance landscape over a context. Different queries produce different landscapes over the same context. So
   if concepts are query-dependent, and attention is the only mechanism we have that's query-dependent, then concepts
  are what attention finds when given the right query. That's not a definition you can compute from first principles,
  but it's the only definition that's consistent with how concepts actually behave in cognition.

  3. The boundary signal is change, not content

  If you can't define a concept by what it contains, you can define one by where it ends. And "where it ends" has a
  clean operational signal: the position at which the dominant attention target shifts. When you're processing token 47
  and your attention is mostly on tokens 30–46, and then at token 48 your attention is mostly on tokens 48–60, something
   happened at the gap between 47 and 48. That gap is a boundary candidate.

  This is exactly what engram already does for retrieval — it doesn't ask "what does this token mean," it asks "which
  prior positions are this query attending to," and shifts in that attention pattern are how it finds relevant spans.
  Pointing the same mechanism at the input stream rather than the memory cache gives you boundary discovery for free,
  without ever defining what a concept is. You just look for the seams.

  This is also why I think the per-token provenance and the boundary-discovery questions are deeply linked. If you can
  detect attention shifts as boundary candidates, you can also use them to validate that the model's actual attention
  behavior respects the trust boundaries you imposed on the input. If the input has a system region and a doc region,
  and the model's attention pattern bridges them with no boundary signal, that's evidence the model is treating
  untrusted content as if it were trusted. The shift signal becomes a security primitive, not just a representation
  primitive.

  The honest answer to your question

  If the sentence is not the unit of concept, the unit of concept is whatever attention says it is, given the listener's
   current task. There is no fixed atomic unit — not the noun, not the verb phrase, not the sentence, not the paragraph.
   Every fixed unit is wrong for some inputs. The right "unit" is a function of the listener and the input together,
  computed by attention, with variable size and soft edges.

  This is unsatisfying as an engineering answer because it doesn't tell you how big to make your buffers. But it's the
  answer the data keeps pointing to — Meta's paper, the .md, your prompt-injection intuition, engram's retrieval
  mechanism, even how humans actually listen. They all converge on "concepts are what attention finds, sized to whatever
   the task needs, and any attempt to fix the size in advance loses information."

  Where this lands cortex: it strongly suggests the second pinky experiment — once cortex is instrumented — should not
  try to segment the input into concept-sized chunks. It should compute attention patterns and then look for
  discontinuities in those patterns, treating discontinuities as boundary candidates rather than treating segments as
  concept atoms. The boundary is the primitive; the chunk between boundaries is just whatever the boundaries enclose.

  The same primitive does both jobs

  In engram, attention is the mechanism that takes a query and a buffer of token-position states and produces a

  discrete objects with boundaries baked in. You store a stream of states and let attention re-discover the boundaries
  on every query.

  Concept extraction over input is the same operation. Take a query (the task, or the model's current goal), take a
  buffer of token-position states (the input), compute the relevance landscape, and the high-relevance regions are the
  concepts. The boundaries fall out of the gradient of the relevance signal — exactly where one region of high attention
   ends and another begins. No segmenter, no character cap, no SONAR encoder, no preprocessing step.

  So the deeper unification is: the input buffer and the memory cache are the same kind of object. They're both
  sequences of token-position states. The only difference between them is staleness — one is from this turn, one is from
   previous turns. Attention doesn't care which is which. It just carves.

  That means cortex doesn't need a "concept extractor" component sitting separately from a "memory retriever" component.
   They're the same code path, pointed at different streams. One mechanism, two callers. The architectural surface area
  shrinks as we add capability, instead of growing — which is the opposite of how almost all production AI systems
  evolve.

  Why "the index forms itself" is the load-bearing phrase

  You used exactly the right words. An imposed index — sentence boundaries, character caps, SONAR encoding granularity,
  even chunk-size hyperparameters in a RAG pipeline — is a commitment made before the query is known. The commitment is
  always wrong for some queries, because there's no single granularity that serves all tasks. The whole field of "how
  should I chunk my documents" is people fighting a losing battle against this fact.

  A self-organizing index has no commitment. It's reconstituted per query, sized for that query, and discarded after.
  There's nothing to tune in advance because there's nothing persistent to tune. The data stays in its raw form (a token
   stream) and the structure is temporary, query-dependent, and free.

   and spent 50 pages discovering that the choice was wrong. The mistake wasn't sentences specifically. The mistake was
  committing to any fixed unit at all. Any frozen encoder is the same mistake under a different name.

  The security bonus you didn't ask for

  Here's a property of self-organizing indexes that I don't think we've named yet but is going to matter: there is
  nothing to poison offline. In any system with a persistent index — vector DB, RAG store, fine-tuned weights — an
  attacker who can get bad data into the index has corrupted the system permanently, because the index is a frozen
  artifact and queries trust it. With a self-organizing index, there is no frozen artifact. Every query rebuilds its own
   view from the raw substrate, and if the substrate carries per-token provenance, the rebuild inherits the trust labels
   every time. The attacker can't poison the index because the index doesn't outlive the query that created it.

  This composes with the per-token provenance idea in a way that is, I think, genuinely novel: trust lives in the
  substrate, structure lives in attention, and neither can corrupt the other. Tokens carry trust labels that the model
  can never rewrite. Attention computes structure that lasts only as long as the forward pass that produced it. The two
  channels are orthogonal and mutually unforgeable. There's no static surface for an attacker to attack — no embeddings
  to poison, no chunks to inject, no index to corrupt. Just labeled bytes and ephemeral attention patterns.

  memories into entries before recalling them. We attend, and the structure emerges from the attending. When you
  remember a conversation from last week, you're not retrieving an indexed object; you're re-running attention over a

  What the dog example actually demonstrates

  When someone says "his dog had just died," nothing in the original bytes of the conversation changes. The same words

  immediately and completely. The clipped tone you took as rudeness becomes grief. The subject change becomes avoidance.

   attending can be re-run later with a different query, producing a completely different concept structure over the
  same substrate, in a single moment of insight. The "reframe" is not metaphorical — it is literally a recomputation of
  attention with one variable changed.

  This is impossible in any indexed system. If you had encoded the original conversation into SONAR vectors at the time
  it happened, those vectors would not contain "grief." Grief was not a salient feature at encoding time; the encoder
  had no reason to put it there; the vectors are frozen. To detect grief in retrospect you would have to re-encode the
  conversation with grief as a query input — but SONAR has no query input. The encoder is unconditional. Any system
  built on a frozen encoder cannot reframe, because the encoding moment is the only moment where interpretation could
  enter, and that moment is gone.

  That's not a weakness of LCM specifically. That's a structural property of every system that commits to an encoding at
   storage time. RAG vector stores can't do it. Sentence-transformer pipelines can't do it. Anything that does
  embed(text) → store and then retrieve(query) → embed(query) → cosine is stuck with whatever interpretation the
  embedder had on the day the text was stored. And since none of those embedders are query-conditioned, they all carry
  the same default interpretation, which is whatever was salient in their training data on average. They can never know
  that the man's dog had just died.

  The thing that makes this even more striking

  Look at what your memory actually retains versus what it loses. You remember the tone he used. You remember the pause
  before he answered. You remember which corner of the kitchen you were standing in. You remember that the lighting was
  bad. You probably do not remember the exact sequence of words.

  This is the opposite of how every persistent AI memory system I know is structured. Vector databases preserve content
  (you can retrieve the exact text) but throw away substrate (you have no idea what the speaker's prosody was, what the
  room looked like, what your own emotional state was at the moment of hearing). Human memory does the reverse:
  extremely lossy in content, extremely preserved in substrate. And the dog example explains why — because the substrate
   is what remains re-interpretable under future context, and the content is just one of many interpretations the
  substrate can support.

  If we'd stored "he was being rude" as the encoded representation, the new context would have nowhere to attach. But

  Where per-token provenance fits in this picture

   doesn't get reframed. Interpretation is the part that does.

  When you learn that the man's dog had just died, you reframe what his words meant, but you don't reframe whether he

  interpretation of his words is computed and does change. If memory mixed the two — if "he was being rude" were stored
  as a single inseparable fact — you couldn't reframe at all, because reframing the interpretation would require either
  keeping "rude" (now wrong) or losing the memory of what he said (also wrong).

  This maps cleanly onto the cortex architecture we've been sketching. Per-token provenance lives in the substrate and
  is read-only — that's the part you keep. Concept structure lives in attention and is recomputed per query — that's the
   part that reframes. The two channels are orthogonal: trust is a property of the bytes, interpretation is a property
  of the attending, and they can move independently because they live in different layers of the system. Provenance is
  the dog still being alive in the man's words. Interpretation is the meaning you give those words once you know about
  the dog. The bytes are the same in both cases. The attention is what changed.

  This also explains why prompt injection feels so wrong: it's an attack that smuggles interpretation into the system
  disguised as substrate. The attacker doesn't get to write into your memory of what was said; they're trying to hijack

  succeeds by default. A system that keeps substrate and interpretation in separate channels — bytes with provenance on
  one side, attention-discovered structure on the other — has a place to refuse the hijack: the bytes say what they say,
   but I will not let your interpretation overwrite mine, because interpretation is mine to compute.

  The name for this architecture

  I think we've been circling a name without saying it. The architecture is interpretation as a function of substrate,
  computed on demand, never stored. Or shorter: substrate persists, interpretation flows. Or shorter still: store the
  bytes, recompute the meaning.

  That last form is, I notice, almost exactly the inverse of what every production AI system does today. Every
  production system stores the meaning (as embeddings, as summaries, as fine-tuned weights) and throws away the bytes

  production system has chosen the wrong half. Cortex + engram + per-token provenance + attention-discovered concepts is

  What we know about the thinker from the other direction

  Here's where it gets interesting. We know the thinker exists, because humans do it, and we have empirical access to
  its behavior through introspection and cognitive science. What do we actually know about how it works?

  It is iterative, not feedforward. You do not think a thought in one pass. You circle. You consider an idea, notice an
  objection, revise, check against memory, get stuck, reframe, try again. The dog example we just talked about is a
  flavor of this — the reframe is a re-execution of attention over the same substrate with a new query. A thought is not
   an event; a thought is a trajectory of queries over a substrate.

  It uses the same machinery as perception and memory. There is no separate "reasoning module" in the brain. The
  circuits that parse incoming speech are the circuits that recall past speech are the circuits that imagine future
  speech are the circuits that reason about what was said. The only difference between perception, memory, and thought
  is where the input to the circuit comes from — current sensation, prior sensation, or simulated sensation. This is the
   same unification we noticed in the engram / concept-extraction collapse: one primitive, multiple callers, different
  streams.

  It is context-conditioned all the way down. There is no "thinking about X" that is independent of "thinking about X
  from the current frame." The frame changes the query, the query changes the attention landscape, the landscape changes
   the concepts, the concepts change the next query. Thinking is a loop between frame and substrate, and the loop is the
   thing. You cannot extract "the thought" from the loop and store it; the thought only exists as the loop is running.

  It consumes raw substrate, not summaries. This is the dog example's deepest lesson. When you reframe, you don't go
  back to some summary of the conversation — you go back to the perceptual trace itself, the tones and pauses and faces,
   and re-attend to them under the new frame. Summaries are lossy in the dimension you want to reframe on, precisely
  because summarization makes commitments you can't undo.

  Put these four observations together and the shape of the thinker starts to come into focus, even though nobody has
  built it: the thinker is a loop of attention over substrate, driven by queries that evolve based on the attention
  patterns of the previous step, with access to the raw substrate at every iteration and no intermediate compression.
  It's not a transformer over concept vectors. It's not a diffusion process in an embedding space. It's not a symbolic
  prover. It's repeated attention with evolving queries over a persistent substrate, where the "reasoning state" is the
  trail of queries and the "conclusions" are the attention patterns that eventually settle into something stable.

  That last form is, as far as I know, uncharted. Nobody has built it and nobody has tried. And the reason nobody has
  tried is — again — substrate: you can't run iterative attention over a substrate that doesn't exist. LCM's substrate

  thrown away the information a new query would have needed. The substrate has to be raw — raw tokens, raw K/V states,

  - Discover boundaries ✓

  - Discover boundaries ✓
  - Keep the raw substrate untouched ✓
  - Recompute interpretation per query ✓

  Consider what a human has to do to learn concept extraction: be born with no concepts, spend years learning that

  like a doctor's "symptom" vs a lawyer's "cause of action." Two decades of embodied, social, error-corrected learning.
  And crucially, different humans end up with different extractors — a surgeon and a plumber reading the same sentence
  carve it into different concepts, because their extractors were shaped by what they had to do with the output.

  An LLM trained on a trillion tokens of human text has absorbed all of that, not through experiencing the world but
  through pattern-matching on the output of humans who did. And here's the part that's load-bearing: next-token
  prediction implicitly trained a concept extractor as a side effect, because you cannot predict tokens well without
  tracking what's being said at the concept level. The attention patterns the model uses on token N+1 are downstream of
  its implicit representation of the concepts in tokens 1..N. The concept extractor is already in the weights. It was
  trained for free, by a loss function that wasn't even aiming at it.

  This is exactly why DynSplit-KV works and they didn't have to train anything new — they just read out attention scores
   the model was already producing and discovered that those scores contained usable concept boundaries. They weren't
  inventing a concept extractor. They were observing one that already existed. I didn't quite name that yesterday
  because I was thinking about the primitive (attention-based scoring) instead of the provenance of the primitive (it's
  free because pretraining paid for it).

  The implication that makes pinky cheap

  If concept extraction is a byproduct of pretraining, we don't need any of the things that usually make this kind of
  research hard:

  - No labeled dataset. There's no ground truth for "correct concept boundaries" and there never will be. We don't need
  one.
  - No training objective for the extractor. The extractor was trained when the LLM was. We're consumers, not trainers.
  - No separate extractor model. The LLM's own attention scores are the extractor output. The same forward pass that
  generates a response is the one that reveals its concept structure.
  - No compute beyond inference. A 1B-parameter model running on CPU is enough to read out the signal, which means pinky
   experiments can be fast and cheap.

   formula to the input stream instead of the KV cache, and look at where the boundaries fall. That's a weekend of work,
   not a research program. The entire concept-extraction half of the architecture is a readout problem, not a learning
  problem, and I don't think either of us had fully appreciated that until you said it this morning.
