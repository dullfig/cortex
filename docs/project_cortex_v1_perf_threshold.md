Articulated by Daniel 2026-05-17. Generated by recognition that cortex perf work could continue indefinitely without an explicit stopping condition — and that engineering instinct will keep optimizing past the point of perceptual user-facing benefit unless a threshold is set externally.

## The core distinction

Cortex has **two concurrent roles** that need to be held simultaneously and not conflated:

**Role 1: v1 critical-path component.**
- Cortex perf currently gates v1 launch (inference must feel responsive; ingestion must complete in reasonable time)
- This role has a **measurable threshold** — once met, cortex stops being the rate-limiter
- Past threshold, effort can redeploy to RingHub polish, memex calibration, system integration

**Role 2: ongoing capability platform.**
- Cortex development never stops, same way Linux kernel development never stops
- Each release is a milestone, not an endpoint
- Post-v1 cortex roadmap remains real and substantial

**The v1 perf threshold determines when cortex stops gating launch — NOT when cortex development stops.**

This distinction matters because without it, "stop optimizing cortex" gets interpreted as "freeze cortex," which is wrong. The work continues; it just stops being on the v1 critical path.

## The v1 threshold itself

Two workloads have very different threshold logic.

### Ingestion (offline / batch) — already past threshold

- Current state (2026-05-17): ~1 hour for BHS corpus (49K tokens, 57 files) unsharded after the wgpu sync-pattern fix (commit 7d9a31a)
- With memex's sharding plan: substantially less
- **No further ingestion optimization needed for v1.** Run-once-plus-incremental workload; just has to finish in reasonable time.
- Exception trigger: when real-time ingestion of new RingHub posts becomes a thing (v2+), revisit.

### Inference (online / interactive Bob) — the binding threshold

| Metric | v1 Threshold | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| **Time to first token (TTFT)** | **< 1.5s** | Feels like "Bob is thinking" — past 3s feels broken |
| **Streaming throughput** | **> 15 tok/sec sustained** | Reading speed is ~5-7 tok/sec; 15 outpaces reading with headroom |
| **Concurrent conversations** | **5 without degradation** | Fits early v1 community (South Bay Coastliners + early adopters); scales later |

Once cortex hits all three, **stop further perf optimization as v1 work.** Move to platform-development mode.

## Why the threshold is set where it is

### The reading-speed test is load-bearing

The dominant Bob-perception metric isn't peak tok/sec — it's whether the stream of tokens arrives **faster than the user can read them.** Once tokens outpace reading, perceived speed stops improving.

- Avg silent reading: 250-300 wpm ≈ 5-7 tok/sec
- 15 tok/sec ≈ comfortably faster than reading
- 30 tok/sec ≈ comfortably faster than reading
- 50 tok/sec ≈ comfortably faster than reading

You can't tell the difference among 15/30/50 from the chat bubble. The user sees text appearing faster than they can consume it; whether it's 2× or 5× faster is imperceptible.

Combined with Bob's terse voice (responses are short — "yeah, you were handling it fine though" is 7 tokens), total response time at 15 tok/sec is sub-second after TTFT. **At 15 tok/sec, Bob feels instantaneous from the member's perspective.**

### The BHS-audience-specific calibration

The target audience is **not coming from Claude/ChatGPT expectations.** They're coming from "I called the chapter contact and got voicemail" expectations. A Bob who's 2× slower than commercial AI still feels like magic. So the perceptual bar is *lower* than engineering instinct suggests.

This matters because engineering instinct will keep pushing for "as fast as possible." For *this* deployment, "fast enough that it doesn't feel broken" is the bar — and that bar is much lower than commercial inference targets.

## How to apply

### When evaluating "should we optimize cortex more?"

1. **Run an actual inference benchmark first** — current numbers we have are for ingestion, not generation. Without an inference benchmark we're guessing. One afternoon of cortex-claude time.
2. **Compare to the threshold table above.**
3. **If above all three thresholds**: stop. The next optimization is invisible to members. Redeploy effort.
4. **If below any threshold**: identify which gap is binding, optimize *only that*, re-measure, stop again when threshold met.
5. **Don't optimize for headroom past the threshold "just in case"** — that's the over-optimization trap. Headroom is already baked into the threshold values (15 tok/sec is 2-3× reading speed).

### When evaluating "is cortex done?"

It isn't, and never will be. *V1-ready ≠ done.* See post-v1 roadmap below.

### When effort redeploys

After cortex hits v1 threshold, attention reallocates toward:
- RingHub polish (the community-facing surfaces; `project_do_justice_to_the_medium.md`)
- Memex calibration (tasks #4 score calibration, #5 multi-shard composition)
- System integration (AgentOS HTTP+SSE contract, push paths, multi-tenant)
- Corpus depth (Harmonizer backlog, interview captures)
- Operational readiness (deployment, monitoring, error budgets)

Cortex perf work continues in its own track, at a healthier pace, not gated by launch deadline.

## The wider engineering pattern

This pin captures an instance of the **"good enough" discipline** that applies generally:

- Discovery-bounded work has no natural stopping point — every improvement is satisfying because it's novel
- **You have to impose the stopping condition externally**, by defining a use-case threshold
- Without that threshold, optimization continues by default — engineers love optimizing
- The discipline of saying "fast enough" is uncomfortable but it's how shipping happens

The five-systems framing makes this tradeoff visible: cortex is **one of five**, and every week past threshold trades against RingHub, memex, AgentOS, ringhub-claude work, corpus building, member outreach. **The portfolio view forces the cost of over-optimization into view.**

This pin's discipline should generalize: each system gets a v1 ship threshold and an open-ended post-v1 roadmap, held simultaneously. Cortex is the first to have it written down explicitly.

## What changes when

**Threshold-revision triggers** (when the v1 threshold itself should be revisited):
- Larger membership than initial v1 estimate (e.g., 500+ active members) → concurrency threshold rises
- Real-time corpus ingestion becomes v1 scope → ingestion threshold rises
- Sustained tok/sec demand pattern significantly above 15 → revisit (probably won't happen)
- Cortex perf regresses for some reason → re-measure and act on the actual regression
- **Major perf fix re-shapes the measurement landscape** → re-benchmark, re-evaluate threshold sufficiency. See "Prototype trigger event" below.

## Prototype trigger event — 2026-05-17 TTFT-cliff investigation (mechanism resolved)

The pattern-of-use for this pin's "what changes when" section played out for the first time on 2026-05-17. The path from disappointing benchmark → premature confidence → falsified hypothesis → definitive root cause → architectural fix-shape took ~8 hours and produced durable engineering knowledge along with strategic clarity.

**The setup** (2026-05-17 afternoon): Inference benchmark produced disappointing numbers — TTFT 22s for 500-token prompts, 22s for 2000-token prompts (same — *the smoking gun for fixed-cost dominance*), decode rate 20 tok/sec. Decode rate met threshold ✅; TTFT failed threshold ❌❌. Initial analysis concluded matmul was the ceiling, with cortex 3-4× behind llama.cpp on decode.

**First strategic options pinned**:
- A: fix cliff + cache prompts + ship (threshold revision needed)
- B: fix cliff + cache prompts + GEMM rewrite (v1 slip)
- C: fix cliff + accept current + ship

**Premature commitment** (2026-05-17 ~6pm): A surface-level diagnosis ("phantom allocator-drain wait — fix by pooling") was made and both pins were updated based on the predicted-but-unverified outcome. This was the wrong discipline; should have waited for empirical verification.

**Empirical result** (same evening, ~9pm): **Pool-the-cache fix hypothesis falsified.** Pooling didn't eliminate the cost; it redistributed it across decodes (≈130ms × 128 decodes ≈ same 17s total), made every individual forward 1.5-3× slower, and triggered a hang after ~1000 successful forwards. Reverted; baseline restored. Pins walked back to "diagnostic validated, fix unknown, investigation continuing."

**Root cause investigation** (2026-05-17 ~10pm): Three rounds of `BlockScratch` and `GpuKvCache` internal instrumentation produced definitive answer.

The 17.5s lives in **`BlockScratch::drop` on second-and-later requests** and is **purely CPU-side NVIDIA Vulkan driver bookkeeping** in `vkFreeMemory`. Explicit `device.poll(Wait)` between readback and drop completes in 19 µs — no GPU work pending. The driver does internal allocator bookkeeping that scales with total live allocator state; each `vkFreeMemory` takes ~1.4s when state is large; 12 scratch buffers × 1.4s = 17s. Documented NVIDIA Vulkan behavior, not a wgpu bug, not a cortex bug — a *call-pattern* bug. Cortex was using `vkAllocateMemory`/`vkFreeMemory` per-request as if they were `malloc`/`free`; they're actually closer to `sbrk` and meant to be called rarely.

**Fix-shape (known, conventional)**: Sub-allocate from a coalesced slab. One `vkAllocateMemory` at engine startup for a large slab; scratch regions are offsets into it; ONE `vkFreeMemory` at shutdown (or never). Industry-standard GPU programming pattern (llama.cpp, CUDA memory pools, every serious GPU codebase). Daniel's framing: *"a mini OS in the GPU doing memory management."* Exactly right.

**Status (2026-05-17 10pm)**: mechanism definitive; fix-shape known and conventional; implementation in progress (Option 1 finishing for bench data; Option 2 = coalesced slab is the durable fix). Per-block scratch refactor touches every shader binding site; estimated 1-2 days. Implementation perf gains pending verification.

**Implication for v1 launch (current)**:

The cliff is no longer "unknown ship blocker." It is a known engineering problem with a known industry-standard fix-shape that requires conventional GPU sub-allocator work. **V1 ship is unblocked at the planning level**; only implementation verification remains.

Strategic option pruning:
- **Option A (ship with threshold revision)**: superseded — cliff has a real fix, no threshold revision needed
- **Option B (GEMM rewrite)**: still valid as v2 work; not v1-critical
- **Option C (accept cliff)**: not viable; superseded by Option D yielding
- **Option D (root-cause the cliff)**: **yielded successfully**; this section documents what it produced
- **Option E (build the sub-allocator)**: now the v1-critical implementation path — 1-2 days estimated, plus a benchmark to verify

**Bonus payoff of sub-allocator work**: same architectural pattern serves resident-weight runtime, multi-tenant scaling, BitNet at scale. The sub-allocator isn't just cliff-fix; it's substrate cortex was going to need anyway. **One investment, two roadmap items addressed.** Combines v1 unblocking with v2+ enabling work.

**Threshold implications**:

| Metric | Threshold | Pre-investigation | Post-investigation expected (pending verify) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decode rate | > 15 tok/sec | 20 ✅ | 20 ✅ (sub-allocator doesn't change decode compute) |
| TTFT 50-tok | < 1.5s | 2.8s ❌ | ~0.5s ✅ |
| TTFT 500-tok | < 1.5s | 22.4s ❌❌ | ~3s ❌ (cliff removed, prefill compute remains) |
| TTFT 2000-tok | < 1.5s | 22.4s ❌❌ | ~12s ❌❌ (cliff removed, prefill compute scales) |

With prefix-caching layered on top: typical 1000-1500 tok prompts with 70-80% cache-hit → effective fresh prefill 200-400 tokens → ~1-2s TTFT for typical workloads, comfortably at threshold.

These remain **expected** values pending empirical verification. The discipline lesson from earlier today is to mark these as predictions, not commitments. Pin updates after the sub-allocator lands + benchmark verifies, not before.

**Strategic state**: v1 ship path is now *known engineering work*, not *unknown research*. Effort estimate 1-2 days for sub-allocator + a day for prefix-cache + re-benchmark cycle. The threshold framework continues to do its job: each cycle (audit → measure → hypothesize → verify → revise) converges toward a decision-quality answer.

**What this entire 2026-05-17 event illustrates about the threshold framework**:

1. **The threshold framework works as intended.** Without the explicit "audit before optimizing kernels" sequencing, cortex might have charged at GEMM/CubeCL based on the original numbers — spending weeks on the wrong problem while the actual blocker (vkFreeMemory cost) sat undiscovered.
2. **Premature pin updates are an anti-pattern.** Earlier I updated both pins based on a hypothesis that hadn't been verified empirically. The discipline going forward: **pin updates should follow empirical verification, not predicted-and-clever-sounding outcomes.** This pin's previous version reflected that mistake; this version reflects the corrected discipline.
3. **Hypothesis-and-empirical-test discipline is load-bearing.** The phantom-work pattern produces good diagnostics; the diagnostics produce hypotheses; **not all hypotheses survive empirical test**. The discipline is to test before committing.
4. **Mechanism finding is durable; fix-shape verification is empirical.** Today produced a durable engineering finding (vkFreeMemory cost scales with allocator state, per-buffer ~1.4s) that will inform every future GPU buffer-lifecycle decision in cortex. The fix-shape (sub-allocation) is industry-standard. The actual perf delivered by cortex's specific implementation is pending verification.
5. **Audit-first sequencing has now paid back three times cleanly** (sync, logits, root-causing the cliff) plus one investigated-and-found-fundamental-engineering-need (the sub-allocator). Pin discipline is still earning its keep — without it we'd have shipped GEMM work that wouldn't have solved this cliff either.

**What needs to happen for v1 ship-decision**:
1. Implement Option 2 (sub-allocator / coalesced slab for BlockScratch) — 1-2 days
2. Run inference benchmark against threshold table; confirm cliff is gone
3. Implement prompt-prefix caching at cortex/AgentOS boundary
4. Re-bench with realistic Bob workloads (1000-1500 tok with prefix-cache hits)
5. Compare to threshold; ship cortex for v1 if at threshold
6. Park GEMM/CubeCL work + sub-allocator extensions to other buffers (cache, weights) as v2 platform work

**Roadmap-promotion triggers** (when a post-v1 item should move to v1-critical):
- Generally: don't promote unless something materially changes about v1 scope
- Specifically: be skeptical of "we should do BitNet now" or "we should do CubeCL now" arguments unless v1 inference is actually failing the threshold
- Recognize the seductiveness of platform work — it's more interesting than RingHub polish, but RingHub is what members touch
